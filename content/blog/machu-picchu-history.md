Machu Picchu gets called a "lost city," which isn't quite right. It was never lost to the people who knew about it — it was simply never conquered, never looted, and never mentioned to the Spanish, who spent decades hunting for Inca gold just a few mountain ridges away.

## Built as a royal retreat, not a city

Most archaeologists now believe Machu Picchu was built around 1450 as a private estate for the Inca emperor Pachacuti, one of the empire's most powerful rulers. Radiocarbon dating of skeletal remains suggests the site was occupied from roughly 1420 to 1530. Rather than a city in the everyday sense, it functioned more like a royal retreat — a seasonal residence with an estimated population of only about 750 people, most of whom were staff, priests, and support workers rather than permanent residents.

Perched at 2,430 meters (7,970 ft) above sea level on a ridge between two mountains, the site's location wasn't an accident. The Inca built it to align with surrounding peaks and astronomical events, and its terraces, water channels, and dry-stone construction — fitted together without mortar — remain remarkably intact more than 500 years later.

## Why the Spanish never found it

When Spanish conquistadors swept through the Inca Empire in the 1530s, they captured and looted the major imperial centers, including Cusco. But Machu Picchu is tucked away on a remote ridge, disconnected from the main road network in a way that made it easy to miss if you didn't already know it existed. Most historians believe the site was quietly abandoned around the time of the Spanish conquest — likely due to disease and the collapse of Inca political structures — rather than being destroyed or captured. It simply emptied out and was reclaimed by the surrounding cloud forest.

## The "discovery" that wasn't quite a discovery

In 1911, American historian Hiram Bingham arrived at Machu Picchu while searching for a different lost city, Vilcabamba. He was guided to the site by a local farmer, Melchor Arteaga, who — like other people living in the valley — already knew the ruins were there. In fact, a Peruvian explorer named Agustín Lizárraga had visited the site nearly a decade earlier, in 1902, and there's evidence local families were living among the ruins and farming nearby terraces when Bingham showed up.

What Bingham actually did was bring Machu Picchu to international attention. He returned in 1912 with backing from Yale University and the National Geographic Society to conduct major excavations, and his photographs and writing turned a site known locally into one of the most famous archaeological locations on Earth.

## Quick facts

- **Built:** around 1450, under Emperor Pachacuti
- **Occupied:** roughly 1420–1530
- **Elevation:** 2,430 meters (7,970 ft)
- **Brought to world attention:** 1911, by Hiram Bingham
- **UNESCO World Heritage Site:** since 1983

## Visiting today

Machu Picchu is now one of the most tightly managed heritage sites in the world, with daily visitor caps and timed entry to protect the ruins. Most travelers reach it via train to Aguas Calientes followed by a shuttle bus, though the multi-day Inca Trail — walking in on some of the same stone paths built for Pachacuti's court — remains the way to arrive the way it was originally meant to be seen.
