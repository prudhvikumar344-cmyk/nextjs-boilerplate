Before the Colosseum, there was Nero — and after Nero, Rome wanted everyone to forget he'd ever ruled.

## Built on a tyrant's private lake

After Emperor Nero died in 68 AD, his successors inherited a city that associated him with excess: he'd seized a huge stretch of central Rome to build himself a private palace complex, the Domus Aurea, complete with an artificial lake. When Emperor Vespasian took power and founded the Flavian dynasty, he drained Nero's lake and built a public amphitheater in its place — a calculated move to hand back to ordinary Romans the land a hated emperor had claimed for himself.

Construction started around 70–72 AD. Vespasian didn't live to see it finished; his son Titus completed and opened it in 80 AD, with further modifications added under his successor, Domitian. Officially it was called the Flavian Amphitheatre, after the dynasty that built it — the nickname "Colosseum" came later, likely from a colossal bronze statue of Nero that once stood nearby.

## An engineering project on a massive scale

The Colosseum could hold somewhere between 50,000 and 80,000 spectators, with typical events drawing around 65,000 — comparable to a modern major sports stadium, built almost 2,000 years earlier with no steel or powered machinery. Its outer wall alone used more than 100,000 cubic meters of travertine stone, fitted together and held with roughly 300 tons of iron clamps rather than mortar. The structure also relied heavily on Roman concrete for its foundations, corridors, and vaulting, and tuff, a volcanic rock, for secondary walls.

Beneath the arena floor was the hypogeum, a network of tunnels and chambers where animals, gladiators, and elaborate stage machinery waited before being lifted into the arena through trapdoors — essentially a backstage area for the empire's biggest spectacles.

## More than gladiator fights

Gladiator contests are what the Colosseum is remembered for, but the venue hosted a wider range of public spectacles: animal hunts, public executions, re-enactments of famous battles, and dramas based on Roman mythology. Historical sources also describe early mock naval battles staged there, though most historians believe those only happened in the amphitheater's earliest years, before the hypogeum was built underneath the arena floor.

## From arena to quarry to ruin

The Colosseum's decline wasn't a single event but a slow accumulation of damage. A major fire struck in 217 AD, and centuries of earthquakes took a further toll — most dramatically in 1349, when a severe earthquake caused the outer south side to collapse entirely. During the medieval period, the building was repurposed as housing, workshops, and even a fortress, and for centuries afterward it was treated as a convenient stone quarry, with material stripped from it to build other structures around Rome.

## Quick facts

- **Commissioned by:** Emperor Vespasian, around 70–72 AD
- **Completed by:** Emperor Titus, in 80 AD
- **Capacity:** roughly 50,000–80,000 spectators
- **Key materials:** travertine stone, Roman concrete, tuff
- **Major restoration completed:** 2016

## Visiting today

Extensive restoration work, completed in 2016, has stabilized the Colosseum and reopened previously inaccessible areas, including sections of the underground hypogeum. It's now one of Rome's most visited sites and a UNESCO-recognized New7Wonder — nearly 2,000 years after Vespasian first drained a dead tyrant's lake to build it.
